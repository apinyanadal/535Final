1. Thesis

Buyers have to be paid to absorb each month's pre-scheduled 30-year bond auction, so the 30-year Treasury yield rises in the five sessions before it, and a short position in long bonds over those five sessions collects that payment.

Every month the Treasury sells $22 to 25 billion of 30-year bonds. The date is set months ahead in a public schedule and the size is announced a week before. Primary dealers are required to bid, and every long-bond investor knows the supply is coming.

Taking that much duration onto a balance sheet in one afternoon is a risk, so the market makes room beforehand: dealers and investors sell or hedge the long bonds they already hold. That selling pushes yields up into the auction. The higher yield is the discount the new buyers are paid for taking the bonds.

The pattern has a name in the research, the auction cycle. Lou, Yan and Zhang (Review of Financial Studies, 2013) show Treasury prices fall in the days before auctions and recover after, even though timing and size are public. It is still in the data:

I also tested whether the yield falls back after the auction. Since 2020 it has not: on average it kept rising for two more sessions. So the thesis trades only the run-up.

Planned trade: short TLT (the 20+ year Treasury ETF) at the open five sessions before each 30-year auction, and buy it back at the open on auction day, before the 1pm result.

3. Tracker

What it measuresFor every 30-year auction since January 2010, the change in the 30-year yield from the close six sessions before the auction to the close the day before. That is the run-up. The excess run-up subtracts what the yield did over an ordinary five-session stretch with no auction in the prior year, so a general rise or fall in rates is not counted as a concession.

4. Wrong if

1 is the main test. Two years of auctions is long enough that one bad month does not set it off.

2 targets the cause. The concession exists because the supply is large; if Treasury shrinks the 30-year auction, the thesis loses its reason. The tracker compares every new size with the previous one.

3 targets crowding. If traders start selling long bonds earlier, the rise moves out of my five-session window. The concession might still exist, but this trade would no longer capture it.

6. Macro environment

Why it holds now. The government is running a deficit of roughly $1.9 trillion a year, so long-bond auctions stay large: $25 billion for each new 30-year issue and $22 billion for each reopening, unchanged at the November refunding. The buyers have also become more demanding. The Fed stopped its Treasury bill purchases in August and its chair wants a smaller balance sheet, so it is not the backstop it was. Dealers' Treasury inventories reached a record above $550 billion in March (Treasury). Every auction has to clear with buyers who want a discount, and that discount shows up in the week before.

The Fed. The Fed raised rates in September, holds at 3.75% to 4.00%, and markets price a likely hike at the December 8 to 9 meeting. Hiking makes the trade stronger: the run-up was largest in 2022 (+10 bp per window). Falling yields work against it. In 2019 and 2020, when the Fed was cutting, the raw run-up was slightly negative (−0.7 and −0.8 bp a window), though it still beat ordinary weeks by about 1 bp. A turn to cuts in 2027 would weaken the trade without ending it.

The tech and AI selloff. A flight to safety pushes long yields down whatever the supply, and a sharp equity rout inside a window can wipe out that month's concession. The same boom adds to the problem from the other side: hyperscalers are issuing record amounts of long-dated bonds, which compete for the same buyers.

Treasury itself. Two policies cut against the trade. Long-end buybacks, doubled in August, take long bonds back out of the market, and shifting new borrowing into bills keeps 30-year sizes flat. A cut to the 30-year auction size is wrong-if condition 2.

7. Method and data

  • Sample. Every nominal 30-year auction since January 2010, new issues and reopenings. TIPS excluded.
  • Run-up. 30-year yield (daily close) on the session before the auction minus the close six sessions before. Excess run-up subtracts five times the average daily change over the prior 252 sessions, leaving out every session that belongs to a run-up window.
  • No look-ahead. Auction dates are published months ahead in Treasury's tentative auction schedule, so the entry date is known before the trade.
  • 20-year auctions, tested and left out. Since 2020 their run-up is no larger than an ordinary week's move over the same period.
  • Post-auction bounce, tested and left out. Since 2020 the 30-year yield has kept rising after the auction, by about 1 bp over the next two sessions.
  • TLT check. Short at the open five sessions before, cover at the open on auction day. Windows that contain TLT's monthly ex-dividend date are skipped, so a dividend never looks like a gain.
